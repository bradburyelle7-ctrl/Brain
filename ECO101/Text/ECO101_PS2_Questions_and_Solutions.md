1) Aman is taking a 4th-year economics course. The class has a 2-person group project that needs a paper and a
presentation. He can produce 1 page per hour or 5 mins of the presentation per hour, assuming he uses his
time eﬃciently (i.e. assuming eﬃcient production).
1a) Barbara can produce 2 pages per hour or 10 minutes of the presentation per hour, assuming eﬃcient production.
Who has the absolute advantage and who has the comparative advantage in paper writing?
Solution: Barbara can produce more pages per hour than Aman and more presentation minutes per hour, soshe
has the absolute advantage in both goods.
To ﬁnd the comparative advantage, we need to work out who has the lower opportunity cost of paper writing.
If they’re not writing the paper, they can work on the presentation. To produce one more page, Aman needs
1 hour, which means he gives up 5 presentation minutes.
To produce 1 extra page, Barbara needs 30 mins, and in those 30 mins she could have produced 5 presentation
mins.
Her opportunity cost of 1 extra page is also 5 presentation minutes.They have the same opportunity
cost, so neither of them has a comparative advantage.
Alternatively, you could calculate it using ratios. I recommend doing it this way: it catches the errors that
are easy to make with intuitive arguments, especially when fractions are involved.

1b) Suppose Aman and Barbara form a group. They each have a total of 10 hours to devote to this project. The
paper needs to be 10 pages long. Who will write the paper and how long will their presentation be?
Solution: To be eﬃcient, the person who starts writing the ﬁrst page should be the one with the lowest opportunity
cost, since they give up the least to produce that page.
In this case it doesn’t matter: neither of them has a comparative advantage in paper writing, soeither one
can write the paper, and the presentation will be 100 minutes long either way.
If Aman spends 10 hours working on the paper, the total presentation time will be 0*5 minutes from him
and 10*10 minutes from Barbara = 100 minutes. If Barbara writes the paper, producing 10 pages takes her 5
hours, which leaves 5*10 mins of presentation from her and 10*5 mins from Aman = 100 mins of presentation.
If they share the paper writing, they still reach the same maximum number of presentation minutes.
1c) Barbara drops the course and Aman ﬁnds a new partner, Chen, who can produce 2 pages per hour or 12 mins
of the presentation per hour, assuming eﬃcient production. Who should write the 10-page paper now?
Solution: Chen also has an absolute advantage in both tasks. Her opportunity cost of 1 extra page is 6 mins of
presentation, which is higher than Aman’s, soit is more eﬃcient for Aman to write the paper.He
takes 10 hours to write it and gives up a total of 10*5 = 50 mins of presentation time to do it. If Chen wrote
the paper, she would give up 10*6 mins = 60 mins to produce the same 10-page paper.

1d) The professor changes the requirement to 15 pages. On presentation day, Aman and Chen’s team submitted
their 15-page paper and did a 50-minute presentation. Were they on their PPF with this choice of paper
length and presentation time? Explain.
Solution: As discussed above, if the team is producing eﬃciently, Aman should start the paper writing. After 10
hours he runs out of time, so Chen steps in to write the remaining 5 pages, which takes her 2.5 hours. With
her remaining 7.5 hours she could produce a (7.5*12 mins =) 90-minute presentation, if the team were working
eﬃciently. However, they only did a 50-minute presentation, which is less than their maximum possible, so
the team is below their PPF and producing ineﬃciently.
1e) For the next project, Aman is assigned a diﬀerent partner–Dov, who can produce 5 pages per hour. Since Dov
is clearly better at paper writing, he should write the paper. Agree, Disagree or It depends? Explain.
Solution: It depends. Dov may have an absolute advantage in paper writing,but until we know how good
he is at presentation preparation, we can’t say who should write the paper.The parts above give
examples that illustrate this.

3c) A consultant looking at the opportunity cost numbers says that Helga should produce only beef and Gerd only
kale. Agree, Disagree or It depends?
Solution: It depends (a typical economist answer, though not always). It depends on the demand for beef (or
kale).
If the demand for beef is very high, once the low (opportunity) cost producer, Helga, has produced all the beef
she can, the extra beef to satisfy the demand will come from the high-cost producer, Gerd.Since Gerd is
also the lowest-cost producer of kale, he ends up producing both goods rather than specializing.
To convince yourself, look at the numerical example in part (b).

Solution: This question asks you to think about the role specialization plays in our lives, and how much that role
depends on circumstances and on what is actually possible.
Choosing a career is choosing to specialize.
Hopefully, you specialize in something you have a comparative advantage in – that is, something you are
relatively good at, given what everyone else in society can do. You can become a professor because you know
that if you ever need your plumbing ﬁxed, you can trade with someone who has a comparative advantage in
that skill. (You could barter services, but money serves as a medium of exchange.)
Whether you become a professor could depend on how conﬁdent you are that you will ﬁnd a plumber when
you need one, and that the terms of trade will make it worth your while to hire them. If not, you may be
better oﬀ doing it yourself.
What makes you so sure that you will ﬁnd a plumber and pay them rather than learn to do it yourself? What
are you implicitly assuming?
From society’s perspective it is more eﬃcient to allocate people to tasks based on comparative advantage
(i.e. to be on the PPF), but that may not be possible: it depends on the extent of the market, people’s
expectations, and larger institutions.
5) The topic of Canadian jobs moving abroad is often in the news. The types of jobs and the industries change.
One example is call-center jobs moving to India. Recently, those jobs have been moving back to Canada, as
discussed in a Globe and Mail article. A CEO in the article explains why his company moved jobs back:
“‘It worked well – for a while,’ Mr. Day said about Primus’s Indian experience, which involved the creation of new jobs
to serve the company’s expanding consumer business in the mid-2000s. ‘We got qualiﬁed, college-educated employees
with accent neutralization training. But over the years we felt the quality of agent started to decline’ and by 2009, ‘my
money wasn’t buying what it was a few years earlier.’”1
How could you explain this declining worker quality using the concepts we have studied in this topic?
Note: there could be many other reasons behind this change. Asking you to look at it through a single lens is
constraining, and may force you to ignore important aspects. The purpose is to develop your ability to apply
course concepts to a particular real-world example. Notice which important aspects feel missing when you focus
on just one concept, and pay attention to why you think it is not the best concept to apply here. Doing this
develops the other course learning objective–appreciating the role of assumptions and the beneﬁts/limitations
of models. Your answer may be diﬀerent from mine, so discuss your reasoning with your classmates and us.
Just use topic concepts.
Solution: One way to look at it is the beef-and-kale example from Q3b, applied within a single country. The
mechanism there was this: once Helga, the low-cost producer, had produced all the beef she could, any extra
beef had to come from Gerd, the high-cost producer, so the opportunity cost of the marginal unit of beef rose.
Here, call-center services play the role of beef, and workers within India play the roles of Helga and Gerd.
Suppose India has a comparative advantage in call-center services relative to Canada and other richer countries
e.g. the US and the UK. If demand for call-center services from India is high, the workers best suited to the
job – the ones with the lowest opportunity cost, the Helgas – select into those jobs ﬁrst. Once all the Helgas
are employed and demand is still high, the Gerds start working at call-centers, and they are not as good at
it. Given the Gerds’ opportunity costs compared to those of Canadian workers, it may now make more sense
to bring some jobs back.
Throughout this reasoning, Canadian workers could have had the absolute advantage in call-center services
all along. But comparative advantage – and hence trade – depends on relative productivities, i.e. opportunity
costs. You can use the numerical examples in the other problem set questions to think through this as well,
e.g. the Eﬃe/Frank/Faizal question.

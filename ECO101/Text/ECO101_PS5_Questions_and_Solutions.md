1) Consider a perfectly competitive market with no externalities. Market demand is given byQD = 65−P while
market supply is given byQS = P−5. Calculate Consumer Surplus, Producer Surplus and Total Surplus.
Solution: We need to ﬁrst ﬁnd market quantity and price. We do that by setting
QD = QS
65−P = P−5
P = 35
⇒Q = 30
To calculate CS we need the intercept of the demand curve on the y-axis, which is65. This makes CS the area
of the triangle with base = 30 (the quantity), height = 65-35 (the diﬀerence between price and the intercept
of the demand curve on the y-axis).
CS = 0.5∗(65−35)∗30 = 450
Similarly, for PS we need the intercept of the supply curve on the y-axis, which is5. This makes PS the area
of the triangle with base = 30 (the quantity), height = 35-5 (the diﬀerence between price and the intercept
of the supply curve on the y-axis).
P S = 0.5∗(35−5)∗30 = 450
Since we have no externalities, we can use the private beneﬁts to calculate the social beneﬁts. No members
of society are aﬀected by the buying and selling other than the consumers and producers, so we can calculate
total surplus using
T S = CS + P S = 900
Consumer surplus is $450, producer surplus is $450, and total surplus is $900.

2b) Can you calculate total surplus?
Solution: Without being told that all beneﬁts and costs accrue only to the parties in the transaction and no one
outside (i.e. no externalities), we couldn’t calculate total surplus. There could be beneﬁts or costs accruing to
people outside the transactions that would need to be counted in total surplus. Another way of saying this is
that social beneﬁt (or cost) could be diﬀerent from private beneﬁt (or cost). The demand and supply curves
just reﬂect private costs and beneﬁts. But in this question, since we are told it is a perfectly competitive
market, we know that one of the assumptions is no externalities, sothat holds here and we can calculate
it.
Market quantity is 23, and total surplus is the area below the MWTP curve and above the MC curve up to
the market quantity.
T S = 0.5∗23∗(60−2.5) = 661 .25
Total surplus is $661.25.

5) Assume we have a perfectly competitive market and no externalities. Remember we only have downward
sloping demand and upward sloping supply in ECO101. Suppose that the marginal cost decreases by $1 for
each quantity produced. How does consumer surplus, producer surplus and total surplus change?
Solution: This is a parallel downward shift of the supply curve. As demand is downward sloping, we know that
the change in market price is going to be less than $1. See the diagram below to convince yourself. Market
quantity bought and sold increases.
Consumer surplus increases by less than a dollar for every unit that was bought before (price is cheaper). In
addition, there are new units bought and the additional CS from those units causes market CS to increase.
If you thought only about the old costs, you would say that producer surplus for previously sold units falls
for the same reason that CS rises–producers are getting less money for those units than they did before. But
remember that costs fall by $1 and price falls by less than $1. This means that PS increases for the previously
sold units too. In addition, you have the increase in PS from the new units sold.
An increase in both means that total surplus increases.

6a) If Oscar had won a spot instead of Anisha what would have been the change in surplus?
Solution: If Anisha is made to give up her slot, surplus goes down by $3675. When Oscar gets the spot, surplus
goes up by $2391.The net change in surplus is -$1284, i.e. surplus decreases by $1284.
6b) What would have been the change in surplus if the spaces had been allocated eﬃciently?
Solution: If the spaces had been allocated eﬃciently, they would have gone to the students with the highest WTP,
which in this case are Nicolas and Greta.The change in surplus from giving the spot to Greta and
taking it away from Anisha is an increase of 4523-3675 = $848.
6c) Suppose the department allows students to exchange spots with each other and doesn’t ask too many questions.
Is there a deal to be made here?
Solution: The problem is not the lottery but the fact that students can’t exchange the spots and take advantage
of gains from trade. In this case, Greta values it at $4523 and Anisha values it at $3675. There is space for
a deal to be made.For example, at any price$3675 < p < $4523 both Anisha and Greta would be
made better oﬀ.
6d) Suppose instead that students on the list settle on a price of $4000. If a student with a spot wants to sell a
seat, they can sell it for $4000 to a student who wants to buy one. Does this reach the eﬃcient allocation?
What is producer surplus from those who sell and what is the consumer surplus from those who buy?
Solution: At a price of $4000, Nicolas’ WTP is higher, so he keeps his spot. Anisha’s is lower, so she sells hers.
Greta’s is higher, so she buys the spot, and Oscar’s is lower, so he is not interested in purchasing one.
Consumer surplus is (4523-4000) = $523 from Greta, and producer surplus is (4000-3675) =
$325 from Anisha.
Notice the total change in surplus is $848, the same as you found in part (b). This is another way you can see
how prices work as a signal to move goods across buyers and sellers to reach the eﬃcient allocation. People
working in their own interest exhaust all the gains from trade by making mutually beneﬁcial trades. The
actual price doesn’t matter for the allocation; it is just a way to determine how the surplus is shared between
buyer and seller.

9) Karen decides that as long as someone is willing to pay more than $100, she’ll sell her couch on Craigslist. She
lists her couch for $300, and the next day someone calls her up and oﬀers her $250. What is her Producer
Surplus if she sells the couch?
Solution: If Karen sells her couch, she will have a producer surplus of $150.Karen brings goods
to the market, so she is a producer. PS is price minus willingness to sell = 250-100 = $150. It doesn’t
matter what she lists the couch for; the price she sells it for is the relevant price.

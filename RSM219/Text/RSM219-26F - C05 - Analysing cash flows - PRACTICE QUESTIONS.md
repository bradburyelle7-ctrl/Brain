Key Concepts 
 Cash Paid for Rent: The actual amount of cash the company has paid for rent during the accounting period. This is what we're trying to calculate. 
 Prepaid Rent: An asset account representing rent payments made in advance for future periods. It appears on the balance sheet under current assets. 
 Rent Expense: The cost of using rented facilities or equipment during the accounting period. This amount is recorded on the income statement. 
 
Understanding the Relationship 
The cash paid for rent can be different from the rent expense recognized in the income statement due to changes in prepaid rent. When you pay cash now, 
you increase prepaid rent. Conversely, as time passes and you use the space, the remaining benefit decreases and this is reflected in decreased prepaid 
rent. As a result, equity decreases, and we recognize rent expense.  
 
Formula to Calculate Cash Paid for Rent 
To find the cash paid for rent during the period, you can use the following formula: 
 Beginning prepaid rent + Cash paid for rent – Prepaid rent “used” (expensed) = Ending prepaid rent 
 Therefore, Cash paid for rent = Ending prepaid rent – Beginning prepaid rent + Rent expense 
 Use the formula: 
 Cash paid for rent = $8,000 - $10,000 + $60,000 = $58,000

Key Concepts 
 Interest Receivable: An asset account representing interest that has been earned but not yet received in cash. It appears on the balance sheet under 
current assets. As we earn interest, interest receivable increases and we recognize the increase in equity as interest income. Remember, this occurs as 
interest is earned. So, interest receivable increases when interest is earned but not yet collected and decreases when cash is received for interest. 
 Cash Received from Interest: The actual amount of cash the company has received from interest during the accounting period. 
 Interest Income: The amount of interest earned by the company during the accounting period. This amount is recorded on the income statement and 
increases net income.  
 
Understanding the Relationship 
The relationship between cash received from interest, interest income, and interest receivable is as follows: 
 When interest income is earned, but cash has not been received, the company records an increase in Interest Receivable. 
 When cash is received for interest, the company records a decrease in Interest Receivable (if it was previously recorded as receivable) or 
recognizes cash for interest income earned and received in the same period. 
 Cash Received from Interest may differ from the Interest Income recognized due to changes in the Interest Receivable balance. 
 
Formula to Calculate Cash Received from Interest 
To find the Cash Received from Interest during the period, you can use the following formula: 
 Beginning interest receivable + Interest earned (i.e. Interest Income) − Cash received for interest = Ending interest receivable 
 Therefore, Cash received for interest = Beginning interest receivable + Interest earned – Ending interest receivable 
 Use the formula:  
 Cash received for interest = $3,000 + $16,000 - $3,700 = $15,300 
 
 
Check Your Understanding 
Why is Cash Received from Interest less than Interest Income? 
 Because some of the interest income earned has not yet been received in cash, increasing Interest Receivable 
What does an increase in Interest Receivable indicate? 
 That additional interest income was earned but not collected during the period. 
 An increase means that more interest income was earned than cash received; thus, cash received is less than interest income.

P11-54. (~ 15 min) 
 
AstraZeneca Plc (USD in millions) 
 
a. Depreciation, amortisation, and impairment are added back to undo the effect it had on the income 
statement. AstraZeneca deducted $3,762 million of depreciation, amortisation, and impairment 
expense in computing net income. Depreciation, amortisation, and impairment are noncash 
expenses, so AstraZeneca did not actually use cash to pay those expenses in 2019. Hence, net 
income is lower than cash from operations by the depreciation, amortisation, and impairment 
amount. This add-back is NOT a source of cash as some mistakenly believe. Cash is generated by 
profitable operations, not by depreciation.  
 
b. Credit sales increase net income and trade receivables, but have no effect on cash flow. An 
increase in trade receivables reflects the fact that net income is higher than cash flow from 
operations. As a result, we subtract the $898 increase in trade receivables from net income to 
compute cash from operations.  It does not mean that the company used up $898 for trade 
receivables.  
 
An increase in inventories means that inventory purchases have exceeded goods sold for the 
period. Cash was used for these purchases (i.e., holding accounts payable constant) and, thus, 
net income is higher than cash flows from operations. Hence, we subtract the $316 increase in 
inventories to net income to compute cash from operations.  It does not mean that the company 
used $316 for its inventory.  
 
c. The increase in trade and other payables and provisions reflects credit purchases in current period 
were more than payments for goods purchased in prior periods. All new purchases were sold (i.e., 
holding inventory constant) and, thus, net income was lower than cash flow (COGS was higher than 
cash paid). Hence, we add back the $868 increase in trade payables to net income to compute cash 
from operations.  It does not mean that the company received $868 for trade payables.  
 
d. Capital expenditures (CAPEX) are necessary for routine equipment replacements and new 
additions, while depreciation expense represents the wear and tear of depreciable assets. In 
general, we should expect net CAPEX to exceed depreciation expense. In 2019, $942 (=979 – 37) 
million was spent on new property, plant, and equipment, but depreciation was likely higher than 
that (Assume that deprecation is one-third of the total depreciation, amortisation and impairment of 
$3,762 million). Hence, net CAPEX was not  enough to cover the wear and tear on the property, 
plant, and equipment for the year.   
 
e. In 2019, AstraZeneca paid $3,592 million in cash dividends (did not repurchase any shares). This 
may indicate that the company is in a mature stage, with strong cash inflows but limited growth 
opportunities. Hence, it returns excess cash to investors.

Chapter 12 | LO 1  
Prepare and analyse common-size financial statements. (pp. 545-550) 
Note: We briefly jump to Chapter 12 to introduce common-size analysis, which is a helpful tool for 
evaluating the financial statements. We will return to Chapter 12 later in the course. 
 
 Common-size analysis is a technique used in financial statement analysis where each line item 
in a financial statement is expressed as a percentage of a base figure. This method allows for 
easy comparison across different periods and between companies of varying sizes by 
standardizing the financial data. 
 Vertical analysis: provides a way to understand the relative significance of each item in a 
financial statement. 
 Income statement: each line item is generally represented as a % of net sales (revenues)  
 Balance sheet: each line item is generally presented as a % of total assets 
 Horizontal analysis: involves comparing financial data across multiple periods to identify trends 
and growth patterns. This method highlights changes in financial statement items over time by 
showing the dollar and percentage increase or decrease from one period to the next.

PART 2: CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK OF FINANCIAL REPORTING  
 
#1 | ASSETS – LIABILITIES = EQUITY  →  ASSETS = LIABILITIES + EQUITY 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
  
Chapter 2 | LO 1  
Describe and construct the balance sheet and understand how it can be used for analysis. (pp. 40 -45) 
 
Recall the key elements of financial statements:  
 Assets are present economic resources controlled by the organization. They are based on past 
events and are expected to provide future economic benefits. Current assets are expected to be 
used up or converted in cash within the next year (or operating cycle, if longer).  
 Liabilities represent an obligation for future outflows based on a past transaction or event. Current 
liabilities represent obligations that are due within the next year (or operating cycle, if longer).  
 EQUITY (or NET ASSETS) = Assets – Liabilities  
 There are two key components of equity:  
 Contributed capital represents net amount of funding contributed directly by 
shareholders. This is commonly done by issuing shares in exchange for funding.  
 Earned capital represents accumulation of amounts earned through company’s ongoing 
activities (e.g., by operating profitably) 
 Notice that earnings (net income or net profits) represent increases in earned capital  
 Income (which includes revenues and gains) represents increases in equity, other than 
related to contributions from shareholders.  
 Expenses represent decreases in equity (other than directly related to shareholders) 
 In this course, we will focus primarily on revenues and expenses before introducing gains and 
losses later.

EXERCISE 2.1d: Liabilities  
Common liabilities include the following (see textbook, pp. 43-44):  
 
Current liabilities:  
Accounts payable: amounts owed to suppliers for goods and services purchased on credit 
Accrued liabilities: obligations for expenses that have been recorded but not yet paid

Deferred (unearned) revenue: an obligation created when the company accepts payment in advance for goods or 
services it will deliver in the future 
Current maturities of long-term debt: the portion of long-term debt that is due to be paid within one year 
 
Non-current liabilities:  
Long-term debt: amounts borrowed from creditors that are scheduled to be repaid more than one year in the future  
Other long-term liabilities: various obligations, such as warranty and deferred compensation liabilities, long -term tax 
liabilities

Common equity balances include the following (see textbook, p. 44):  
Contributed capital:  
Share capital: the par value or stated value of shares issued to the primary owners of the company   
Contributed surplus: amounts received from the primary owners in addition to the par value or stated value of 
the common shares 
Treasury shares: the amount paid to reacquire the company’s own common shares 
 
Earned capital:  
Retained earnings: the accumulated earnings that have not been distributed to shareholders as dividends  
Accumulated other comprehensive income or loss: accumulated changes in equity that are not reported in the 
income statement 
 
Share capital is often reported with labels 
like “common shares” or “capital stock”.  
Contributed surplus is also reported as 
“additional paid-in capital”.

“The objective of general purpose financial reporting is to provide information about the 
reporting entity that is useful to current and potential investors, lenders, and other creditors in 
making decisions about investing in equity or debt instruments and providing or settling loans or other 
forms of credit. These users need information that is useful for assessing the amount, timing, and 
uncertainty of future cash flows. They also need information for evaluating how effectively and 
efficiently management and governing boards have discharged their stewardship duties over the 
entity’s resources”. (p. 24) 
 
Fundamental qualitative characteristics:  
 Financial information is useful when it is both 
 Relevant: capable of making a difference in the decision of users (having predictive value, 
confirmatory value, or both); and  
 Faithfully represents economic phenomena in words and numbers (being complete, 
neutral, and free from error).  
 Materiality is considered from the perspective of users. Information is “material” if its omission or 
misstatement could reasonably be expected to change the economic decisions of users (e.g., 
investors, creditors, etc.).

Process for understanding the impact of specific decisions, transactions, and events:  
 Use the basic accounting equation to analyze the impact of transactions, events, and decisions 
on the financial statements. You can use the following steps:  
1. Deeply understand the transaction, event, or decision. What are the motivations or 
intentions behind it? What are the contractual or legal implications? Focus on the 
underlying economic and legal features and implications; don't worry about "accounting" at 
this stage. It is helpful to visualize what is "given up" and what is "received" in a 
transaction. If some part of the transaction happens in the future, then drawing a timeline 
can be very helpful. 
2. Map the transaction, event, or decision to the accounting equation. Remember the 
key definitions of all financial statement elements and work from left to right (i.e., start with 
assets, then consider liabilities, and finally review the impact on residual equity – if any).  
 Transactions are recorded as they happen. They are also reviewed at the end of each reporting 
period and adjusted as needed.

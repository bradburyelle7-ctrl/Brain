Chapter 11 | LO 1 
Explain the purpose of the statement of cash flows and classify cash transactions by type of business 
activity: operating, investing, or financing. (pp. 482-487) 
 
 The purpose of the cash flow statement is to describe the changes in the cash balance and 
explain the sources and uses of cash based on business activities.  
 The statement of cash flows helps us assess organization's financial health, including the 
following:  
 Ability to generate future cash flows 
 Ability to meet its future obligations and pay dividends 
 Company's need for external financing  
 Earnings quality (and differences between earnings and cash flows from operations)  
 Cash flows tend to follow general patterns based on the stage in the organization's life cycle:

#2 | Cash flows are classified based on activity: operating, investing, or financing.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
  
Chapter 11 | LO 1, 4 
 Explain the purpose of the statement of cash flows and classify cash transactions by type of 
business activity: operating, investing, or financing. (pp. 482-487) 
 Construct the investing and financing activities sections of the statement of cash flows. (pp. 498 -
507) 
 
Sources (inflows) and uses (outflows) of cash map to the following types of activities:  
 Cash flows from operating activities (CFO) → payments and receipts relating to:  
 Buying and selling products (i.e. inventory) and providing services  
 Buying and using products and services for core operations (e.g. labour, rent, etc.), including 
expenditures that relate to administrative functions, research and development, taxes, etc.  
 
 
 Cash flows from investing activities (CFI) → payments and receipts relating to:  
 Buying and selling long-lived assets that support core operations (e.g. PPE) 
 Buying and selling financial assets (e.g. shares and debt of other companies)  
 
 
 Cash flows from financing activities (CFF) → payments and receipts relating to: 
 Raising money from investors by issuing shares  
 Distributing earnings by paying dividends  
 Borrowing money and repaying debt 
 
IFRS provides more flexibility over classifying interest and dividends:

We can estimate cash amounts paid/received using the income statement and balance sheet  
 Cash received from customers = revenues – increase in A/R + increase in unearned revenues 
 Cash paid to suppliers = COGS + increase in inventory – increase in A/P 
 Cash paid to employees = wages expense – increase in wages payable 
 Cash paid for other operating costs = other operating expenses + increase in prepaids – 
increase in accruals 
 Cash paid for interest = interest expense - increase in interest payable  
 Cash paid for income taxes = income tax expense – increase in income tax payable 
 
Please note that textbook formulas/examples on pp. 490-491 refer to “net income” in calculating cash 
flows. Please note that this refers to net income impact of specific transactions, such as:  1) revenues 
for cash received from customers; 2) COGS for cash paid to suppliers; and 3) wages expense for 
cash paid to employees.

between the two values. Key reconciling items include the following:

#6 | Free cash flows = operating cash flow – net capital expenditure (most common definition) 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Adame, et al (2023) reported the following key ideas in a paper titled “Free Cash Flow Disclosure in Earnings 
Announcements” 
❑ Free cash flows are a critical component of finance theory, and are used extensively in valuation   
❑ Disclosure of free cash flows is voluntary and there is no “GAAP” definition of “free cash flows”  
❑ Disclosure of free cash flows is growing from about 10% of S&P 1500 in 2004 to about 20% in 2016 
❑ Free cash flows disclosed don’t reflect definitions used in finance theory 
❑ Most common definitions: CFO – Capital Expenditure (noted in 40% of disclosures) and another 21% 
modify the basic definition by adjusting CFO (e.g. removing irregular items) or adjusting capital expenditure 
(e.g. by including proceeds from sales of assets and therefore focusing on ‘net’ capital expenditure)

Process for understanding the impact of specific decisions, transactions, and events:  
 Use the basic accounting equation to analyze the impact of transactions, events, and decisions 
on the financial statements. You can use the following steps:  
1. Deeply understand the transaction, event, or decision. What are the motivations or 
intentions behind it? What are the contractual or legal implications? Focus on the 
underlying economic and legal features and implications; don't worry about "accounting" at 
this stage. It is helpful to visualize what is "given up" and what is "received" in a 
transaction. If some part of the transaction happens in the future, then drawing a timeline 
can be very helpful. 
2. Map the transaction, event, or decision to the accounting equation. Remember the 
key definitions of all financial statement elements and work from left to right (i.e., start with 
assets, then consider liabilities, and finally review the impact on residual equity – if any).  
 Transactions are recorded as they happen. They are also reviewed at the end of each reporting 
period and adjusted as needed.

Also on August 31, THL declared and paid $1,000 in dividends. This reduces cash (an asset), and decreases retained earnings ( earned equity) 
because dividends represent a distribution of prior earnings to shareholders. No contributed equity is affected, as dividends are a return of 
profits. 
On August 31, THL received a $1,000 deposit from a customer relating to a future delivery. The cash asset increases by $1,000 while a liability 
(a customer deposit) is created, reflecting THL’s obligation to deliver products later. This contract liability is called “unearned revenue” or 
“deferred revenue”. Since the revenue is not yet earned, there is no impact on earned or contributed equity at this point.  
 
The remaining $1,500 is not recorded because the contract remains executory (or mutually unexecuted) with respect to that por tion. Under an 
executory contract, neither party has fully performed their respective obligations - THL has not yet delivered the product, and the customer has 
not yet paid the remaining balance. In accrual accounting, revenue (and the corresponding increase in earned equity) is recog nized only when 
performance obligation is satisfied. Therefore, the $1,500 will not be recorded until THL fulfills its obligation by delivering the product on 
September 10th.

When THL originally paid for rent on August 1st, it recorded a $1,000 prepaid rent asset. This represented the right to use the space during 
August. As days passed, the economic benefit was consumed, resulting in fewer remaining days to use the space in August. At the end of 
August, there is no remaining benefit - as the month has passed. The entire $1,000 benefit associated with the prepaid rent has been 
consumed. This consumption reduces the prepaid rent asset by $1,000. Because liabilities remain unchanged, equity decreases by the same 
amount. The $1,000 decrease in earned equity is recorded as retained earnings, and also as rent expense on the income statement. The rent 
expense reflects the use of the prepaid benefit over the rental period. Therefore, the decrease in the prepaid asset directly  translates into lower 
equity and an expense on the income statement.  
THL borrowed $6,000 from the bank on July 31 and didn’t pay any interest during August. After all, interest for August is due on September 1. 
Even though the payment is due on September 1, THL has an existing obligation to make the payment. Therefore, an interest payable liability is 
increased by $50. Since no cash (asset) is reduced at this time and liabilities increase by $ 50, the net effect is a $50 decrease in equity (assets 
minus liabilities). This decrease in earned equity is recorded as a reduction in retained earnings , and on the income statement as interest 
expense. Thus, even though no cash payment occurs, the accrual of interest creates a liability and recognition of interest expense.

Debits and Credits: Debits and credits come down to us from the merchant bookkeeping of 
Renaissance Italy. At first the terms were personal, because ledgers tracked people (a debtor owed 
the business, and a creditor was owed by it). As accounting grew to cover impersonal accounts like 
cash and inventory, those meanings faded into pure convention: 
 By convention, assets hold debit balances while liabilities and equity hold credit balances.  
 Used as verbs, debits increase assets while credits decrease them. The reverse is true for 
liabilities and equity: credits increase liabilities and equity, while debits decrease them. 
 Revenues increase equity, so they have credit balances. Expenses decrease equity so they 
have debit balances.

Chapter 2 | LO 6 
 Use journal entries and T-accounts to analyse and record transactions (pp. 57-65) 
 
Journal entries:  
 Each journal entry is a dated record of a single financial event written as debits and credits 
(e.g., a transaction such as a sale, or an adjustment such as recording depreciation ).  
 Journal entries were used as early paper journals, and it remains how accounting software works 
today. As convention, each entry includes the date of the event in a column on the left, which is 
what places it in chronological order. All accounts being debited are written first, against the 
left margin. All accounts being credited are written afterwards and indented to the right.  This 
indentation is the visual cue that an account is being credited, so the format separates debits from 
credits without labeling every line. The debit and credit amounts must total the same; an entry that 
doesn't balance is incomplete. A short description of the event is usually written just beneath the 
entry. 
 
T-accounts:  
 Each T-account shows the activity of a single account in one place, gathering every debit and 
credit impacting the account. Named for its "T" shape, it has the account title across the top, with 
debits collected on the left and credits on the right. The difference between the two sides is the 
account's ending balance. 
 Whereas journal entries list events in the order they happen, T-accounts re-sort those same debits 
and credits account by account, so each one can be totaled to find its balance.

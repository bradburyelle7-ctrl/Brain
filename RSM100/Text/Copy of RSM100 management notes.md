● Economics - a social science that analyzes the choices people and governments make in allocating scare 
resources  
● Economic Problem of Scarcity - refers to the gap between limited (or scare resources) and limitless wants  
○ This forces people to make decisions around efficient resources allocation so that basic needs and 
additional wants are satisfied  
● Macroeconomics - study of a country’s overall economic issues  
○ Addresses such issues as how an economy as a whole uses its resources and how government 
policies affect people’s standard of living  
● Microeconomics - study of small economic units, such as individual consumers, families, and businesses   
○ Individuals weigh on the benefits and costs related to routine purchases before making a decision, 
all with the desire to maximize benefits and minimize costs 
● Demand - refers to the willingness and ability of buyers to purchase goods and services at different prices 
○ Driven by a number of factors that influence how people decide to spend their money, including 
price and consumer preferences  
■ In general as the price of a good or service goes up, people buy smaller amounts  
● As price rises, quantity demanded declines  
● Demand curves typically slope downward, meaning that buyers will purchase 
greater quantities of a good or service as its price falls  
■ Demand curve shifts: 
 
 
 
 
■ Changes in household income also change demand  
● Consumers have more money to spend, business can sell more 
○ Demand curve shifts to the right  
● Income falls, fewer products are made 
○ Demand curve shifts to the left  
● Supply - the amount of goods and services for sale at different prices  
○ Supply curve shows the relationship between different prices and the quantities that sellers will 
supply or offer for sale, regardless of demand  
■ As price increases, other things remain constant, a producer becomes more willing and 
able to supply the good  
■ Movement along the supply curve is opposite of movement along the demand curve 
● As prices rise, quantity of sellers are willing to supply also rises  
● As prices lower, the quantity supplied decreases

1. Business Ownership 
Sole proprietorship 
● One owner; owner and business are not legally separate 
● Easy to start/control, owner keeps profits 
● Main downside: unlimited liability → owner can be personally responsible for business 
debts 
Partnership 
● 2+ people operate a business as co-owners 
● Benefit: combine money, skills and expertise 
● Downside: share profits/control + potential disagreements 
● Khan example: chef + businessperson → complementary skills 
Corporation 
● Separate legal entity from its owners (shareholders) 
● Advantages: limited liability, can raise money by selling shares, perpetual life, easier to 
grow/recruit 
● Disadvantages: more regulation/complexity, less flexibility, corporate profits taxed + 
dividends may also be taxed at shareholder level 
Not-for-profit 
● Organization whose primary purpose isn't distributing profits to owners 
● Can still earn revenue/surpluses

3. Franchising 
Franchisor = owns brand/system 
Franchisee = pays to operate using that brand/system 
Example: Tim Hortons 
--- Page 23 ---
Benefits to franchisee: established name/trust, proven business model, training/support, 
cheaper supplies from bulk purchasing, known market/performance history, generally less 
uncertainty than starting from scratch. 
Disadvantages: expensive initial fee + royalties, less creativity/control, must follow corporate 
rules/products, dependence on franchisor. 
Trade-off: less freedom → established brand + support + proven system 
 
4. Financing a Business 
Seed capital = initial money needed to launch a business. 
Debt Financing = BORROW 
Examples: bank loans, credit cards, finance companies, family/friends. 
+ Keep ownership/control; interest generally tax-deductible 
− Must repay principal + interest; too much debt → bankruptcy/default risk 
Equity Financing = SELL OWNERSHIP 
● Angel investor: individual investing their own money 
● Venture capitalist: professional group/fund investing in businesses 
+ No normal loan repayment/interest obligation 
− Give up part of ownership, future upside and potentially control 
⭐ Khan bonus-question idea: Preferred shares can sometimes behave like debt. For 
example, cumulative dividends + redemption features can create debt-like payment obligations 
even though preferred shares are equity.

7. Coach Case 
Problem: too much discounting + too many outlets → weakened Coach's perceived 
value/exclusivity and created an “outlet feel.” Shares fell significantly. 
Turnaround: closed underperforming stores, changed leadership, refreshed products, targeted

Expectancy theory of motivation: VICTOR VROOM 
Think of expectancy theory as asking three questions before someone feels motivated: 
1. Effort → Performance: “If I try hard, can I actually do well?” 
○ This is the “can I do it?” part. 
○ Example: If I study 5 hours, do I believe that will actually improve my test

2. Performance → Reward: “If I do well, will I actually get the reward?” 
○ This is the part your notes make a bit confusing. 
○ Example: If I get the highest sales numbers, will my manager actually give

3. Value of Reward: “Do I even care about the reward?” 
○ Example: A $500 bonus might strongly motivate one employee, while an 
extra vacation day might matter more to another.
